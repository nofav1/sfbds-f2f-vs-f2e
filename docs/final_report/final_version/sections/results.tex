\section{Experimental Results}

All counts below are pair expansions on cost-clean solved queries. Win counts and Holm \(p\)-values are those in Tables~\ref{tab:headline} and~\ref{tab:maze-factors} (from the frozen study CSVs). A* expansions are states and are not mixed into these comparisons. On every official instance in this matrix, F2F, F2E, and successful A* agreed on solution cost, and none of those queries timed out. That is empirical coverage of these maps, not a general optimality theorem.

\subsection{Map Families}

Table~\ref{tab:headline} and Figure~\ref{fig:headline} summarize the geography families. Open \(128\times128\) and the \(1\times512\) corridor are all ties (\(0/30\) untied). That does not mean F2F never helps; it means these sizes do not separate the two bounds.

Perfect mazes do separate. On maze 127, F2F expanded fewer pairs on \(22/30\) maps (8 ties, Holm \(p=4.77\times10^{-7}\)), median expansion saving \(3.8\%\). On maze 255 the count is \(26/30\) (4 ties, Holm \(p=2.98\times10^{-8}\)), median saving again \(3.8\%\). Figure~\ref{fig:maze-scatter} shows the same pattern query by query: points lie on or below the equality line, and the F2F saving is a small fraction of a large pair-expansion count.

Nested random is weaker. At \(64\times64\) and \(30\%\) obstacles (seed 110, 1228 blocked cells), F2F expanded fewer on \(13/30\) maps (Holm \(p=0.0002\)); median saving is \(0\%\). The \(128\times128\) geography prefixes at \(10/20/30\%\) do not reach \(n_{\mathrm{untied}}\ge 10\). Nested configs with other seeds are not pooled with this row; they are reported as factors.

\subsection{Map Factors}

Table~\ref{tab:maze-factors} and Figure~\ref{fig:maze-factors} hold maze size fixed and change one factor at a time. Raising the Manhattan floor on maze 127 (seed 141, min MD 90) yields \(15/30\) F2F-fewer, so a longer query at this size did not strengthen the maze effect. Opening \(50\%\) of leftover inter-room walls, with the same endpoints as the unbraided maze, drops the count from \(22/30\) to \(12/30\) on maze 127 and from \(26/30\) to \(11/30\) on maze 255. Loops make the two heuristics agree more often, not less.

Nested random remains seed- and density-specific. Table~\ref{tab:nested} lists every nested prefix with \(n_{\mathrm{untied}}\ge 10\). Seed 210 at \(64\times64\), \(40\%\) and \(45\%\) (1638 and 1842 obstacles), gives \(16/30\) and \(14/1/15\). Those maps are not the seed-110 \(30\%\) instance, so they are not a paired density step on the same grids. On \(128\times128\), the first testable slice is \(45\%\) (seed 220: \(11/30\); seed 221 at MD 28: \(13/30\), \(17/30\), \(17/30\) at \(45/47.5/50\%\)). A second seed-221 run keeps the same density list but draws new queries at min MD 48 (realized \(n=20\)); \(n_{\mathrm{untied}}<10\), so there is no \(p\)-value. Those endpoints are not a subset of the MD-28 thirty. The \(64\times64\) prefixes at \(50\)--\(52\%\) (seed 212) are likewise too tied for a \(p\)-value.

\subsection{Secondary Cost}

Siag et al.\ report large F2F overhead when F2F is a min over the opposite OPEN \cite{siag2023socs}. Our setting is one pairwise Manhattan call per inserted or improved pair. Those are different cost stories; the numbers in this subsection do not refute the two-frontier overhead result.

Figure~\ref{fig:strength} compares both pair bounds on every pair that SFBDS actually scores for OPEN insert or improve. F2E's bound is never strictly stronger on any recorded pair. Open 128 is \(100\%\) equal, which matches the all-tie expansion table. On perfect mazes F2F is often strictly stronger (\(68\%\) of maze-127 pairs, \(90\%\) of maze-255 pairs). Braid drops that share to \(27\%\), in line with the extra ties. Nested \(64@30\%\) (seed 110) and \(64@45\%\) (seed 210) are different maps; both sit near \(56\)--\(59\%\) F2F-stronger. The figure is a mechanism check, not a ranking of savings by correlation.

Median generated-child counts move with expansions: they tie on open and corridor, and F2F is slightly lower on mazes (maze 127: \(6635\) vs.\ \(7055\); maze 255: \(27193.5\) vs.\ \(28425.5\)). Peak OPEN does not track the same way: maze 127 medians are 18 (F2F) and 15 (F2E). Full generated and peak-OPEN medians are in the appendix.

Runtime is not co-primary. On the timed maze-127 rerun (five wall-clock repeats, same seed and queries as the expansion maze), among the 22 expansion-untied queries F2F was faster on all 22, median runtime ratio \(0.885\). That is a check on this cheap-\(h\) maze, not a claim that F2F is faster in general.

The offline eval-cost model charges \(\beta\) seconds per recorded heuristic evaluation and does not re-run search. On maze 127, maze 255, and nested \(64@30\%\), F2F never issued more evaluations than F2E, so raising \(\beta\) cannot reverse the cost order by making heuristics expensive. Figure~\ref{fig:evalcost} plots the median \(T_{\mathrm{F2F}}/T_{\mathrm{F2E}}\) against \(\beta\) on a log \(x\)-axis, which omits \(\beta=0\); that point is in Table~\ref{tab:evalcost-beta0} (maze 127 median ratio \(0.934\)). There is no crossover to F2E on the tested range. The model rescales recorded Manhattan evaluations; it is not a substitute for an expensive online heuristic, and it does not change which pairs the search expands.
